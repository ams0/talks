Bytes. Words. The compromise

Short sequences. Fixed vocabulary. Unseen words welcome.

Additional context: Letters: nothing to learn, enormous sequences. Words: short sequences, and agentgateway isn't in the dictionary. Subwords are the trade.

35 seconds. Left, right, middle — in that order.

"Why not just use letters? Because your vocabulary is two hundred and fifty-six and every sentence becomes enormous — and attention cost grows with the square of sequence length, so that is ruinous. Why not whole words? Because the vocabulary is unbounded. New words arrive every day — agentgateway, llm-d, your internal service names — and a word-level model simply cannot represent them. Subwords are the compromise: a fixed vocabulary that can still spell anything it has never seen."

If challenged on "256": byte-level, so 256 is exactly right — not an alphabet, the byte range. That is also why these models can handle any script on earth without a special case.

Models. Tools. Agents

One gateway. Three kinds of traffic.

60 seconds. Start of the gateway act. Slow down.

"Here is the thing most LLM gateways get wrong. They sit in front of chat completions and call it done. But an agent does not mostly talk to a model — it talks to tools, over MCP, and to other agents, over A2A. And a completion writes you a bad paragraph. A tool call drops a table. The dangerous traffic is the middle lane, and almost nobody has a policy enforcement point in front of it."

agentgateway handles all three in one data plane: Rust, one binary, donated by Solo.io to the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation. Envoy AI Gateway and kgateway are fine alternatives — the argument is the shape, not the vendor.

agentgateway · tool authorization

Authorize the action

Allow search. Deny deletion. Check every call.

65 seconds. This is the slide the gateway act exists for.

"This is the control that matters for tool access. Not 'may this agent use MCP' — but 'may this agent call delete_customer, given these claims, in this environment'. Written in CEL, evaluated in the data plane, on every single call. Your agent is a program that writes its own next action. You would not give a junior engineer unrestricted production credentials on day one, and you should not give it to something that hallucinates."

If asked how tools get discovered: the gateway can federate several MCP servers behind one endpoint and filter the tool list it advertises — so the agent never even learns that delete_customer exists. That is better than denying it at call time.

Carry the identity

Audit who acted, not only what happened.

35 seconds.

"The single most common thing I see in production agent stacks is a long-lived API key in an environment variable, shared by every user of the agent. Which means your audit log says 'the agent did it'. Useless. What you want is the human's identity carried through the agent, verified at the gateway, exchanged into a short-lived credential, and presented to the tool — so the log says who, not just what."

JWT, OIDC, OAuth token exchange, SPIFFE workload identities. All standard, all boring, none of it specific to AI — which is the reassuring part.

"Look at the bottom bars. They are the same. Seventy-one gigabytes of weights, read out of memory, in both cases. Now look at the top bars. On the left, four and a half petaflops of arithmetic. If someone wants the ratio: prefill runs at ~62,000 FLOP per byte read, decode at 2. The card breaks even around 1,250. One is fifty times over, the other six hundred times under.

Every operational consequence follows from this slide: why your latency dashboard needs two numbers, why a bigger GPU did not help, why there is a whole project devoted to running these on separate machines.

llm-d · prefill/decode disaggregation

Separate when it pays

Prefill for compute. Decode for memory. KV crosses the network.

Additional context: Two deployments, two sets of knobs, one pool in front. Earn this — do not start here.

55 seconds.

"Once you accept that prefill and decode are different machines, the conclusion is obvious: stop making them share a GPU. Prefill pods sized for raw compute, with almost no KV cache. Decode pods sized for memory bandwidth and capacity, with an enormous one. The KV cache travels between them over RDMA. Specialization can improve utilization at scale, while adding network and operational complexity."

Be honest: this is the right answer at scale and the wrong answer for almost everyone in this room. Cache-aware scheduling — the green box on the left — is where the money is and where the risk is not.

On one GPU, the cheap version of this is chunked prefill, which slices a long prefill and interleaves it with everyone else's decode steps. Check support and defaults for your vLLM version and configuration.

60 seconds. The caption is the advice; the panel is the proof it is only config.

"The diagram before this said prefill and decode want different machines. This is that, as deployments. Four prefill replicas tuned for raw compute with almost no KV cache. Twelve decode replicas tuned for bandwidth with an enormous one. The KV blocks travel between them over RDMA. And one InferencePool in front, so the caller still sees a single endpoint."

Then the honest ordering, which is the real takeaway: path one is cache-aware scheduling — reusing warm prefixes while balancing load; its benefit depends on workload and cache hit rate. Path three is wide expert parallelism for MoE at scale.

55 seconds. Point at the hatching, then at the teal.

"Your request does not wait its turn and then get served. It joins a batch that is rebuilt on every single forward pass, next to total strangers. On the left, the old way: four requests start together, and because the batch leaves together, three of them sit there computing padding. On the right, the moment a sequence emits its stop token the slot is free, and the scheduler admits someone else on the very next pass."

The consequence worth saying out loud for the SREs: your tail latency is a property of your neighbours. "It was fast in staging" is not evidence of anything.

Admission is a memory decision — do I have free KV blocks — not a queue position.

Prefix caching

Reuse the prefix

Illustrative latency · cached work helps only when your request reaches it.

55 seconds. This is where the two halves of the talk meet.

"Blocks are keyed by a hash of their contents and everything before them. Your system prompt, your tool schemas, your project instructions — byte-identical on every turn. So on turn seven, the prefill you actually pay for is the delta. The illustrative 900-to-12 ms comparison shows the potential shape of that benefit, not a measured guarantee. Free gift for the room: a timestamp or session ID at the top of a system prompt invalidates every block after it and silently destroys your hit rate. Put volatile content at the end.

60 seconds. The grids do the work — just point.

"Here is the idea that broke the eight-point-nine-millisecond floor. In a dense model every parameter participates in every token, so decode is hostage to the total size of the thing. In a mixture of experts, a small router picks a handful of experts per token, and only those get read out of memory. The model on the right can have ten times the parameters of the one on the left and still be cheaper to decode — because decode is a bandwidth problem, and you just cut the bandwidth bill."

Say the model change out loud, or someone will ask. Everything up to here was Llama 3.3 70B, which is dense — the 8-of-256 grid on the right is a DeepSeek-shaped model, not this one. Phrase it as "and this is where you would stop running Llama": hundreds of billions of parameters, only tens of billions active per token. That ratio is most of why serving costs collapsed over the last two years.

The catch, if asked: every expert still has to be resident in HBM somewhere, so you need either a very large box or expert parallelism across many GPUs — llm-d's third path. You trade memory capacity for memory bandwidth.
