# Beyond the Clouds — delivery script

ADA AI Leadership Day · Matrix One, Amsterdam · 3 September 2026
45 min: **22 min content, 23 min debate** — see the timing note below. ~80 founders and directors, non-technical.
Quoted lines are verbatim — say those. Everything else is a cue, not a paragraph to recite.

**Slides that build.** 19, 34, 38, 39, 40, 43, 44 and 45 reveal one item per click. Right arrow (or
space, or click) brings out the next item, then moves on once they are all out; left arrow steps back
through them. **Down/Up jump whole slides** if you want to skip a build — useful when you are behind.
Printing (P) shows everything regardless. Rehearse these eight: the rhythm is different from a static
slide, and the temptation is to talk ahead of the reveal.

**Timing — read this before you present.** The deck is 47 slides + sources. Slide 3 states the contract
out loud (20–25 min, interrupt freely, save the big ones), so the session is explicitly **20–25 min of
talk and 20–25 min together**.

Delivered in full the deck runs **~29 min**, which overruns even the generous half. Two workable versions:

- **Full argument (~29 min)** — the debate still gets ~16 min, and the provocation slide holds a room
  for 16 min comfortably. Only do this if the organisers are relaxed about the split.
- **On time (~24 min)** — cut **14, 16, 18, 31** and deliver 9 as a ten-second aside off slide 8.
  Slides 8 and 9 now carry the "this is structural" evidence that 15 and 16 used to carry, so losing
  16 costs you very little.

**Cut order if you are behind on stage: 14 → 18 → 31 → 39 → 27.** Never cut 38, and never cut 42.
The exercise (43) comes out of the discussion half, not the talking half — cut it only if you have
blown through both.

## 00 Opening — 3 min (slides 1–6)

**1 · "And now for something completely different." ★new** Have this up as the room comes in and
sits down, and do not advance until you actually begin. It costs three seconds, earns a laugh from half
the room, and tells the other half this is not a vendor keynote. Say the line, then go straight to the
title — **do not explain the joke and do not add a second one.**

**2 · Title.** One line, then move — the contract is the next slide now. "I build AI infrastructure
for a living. Today I want to argue that the most important AI decision you'll make this year isn't
which model — it's whose jurisdiction you run it in."

**3 · How this session works. ★new** 30 seconds, and mean it. "Two things before I start. First,
interrupt me — if a number looks wrong or something doesn't land, say so there and then, you're not
being rude. Second, if it's a big one, hold it. There's a slide at the end with five things I believe
and you might not, and that's where I want to spend the second half."
**Rule 2 is the one that changes the room.** A leadership audience will sit politely silent for
twenty-five minutes unless you explicitly licence them not to. Say it, then **honour it** — take the
first interruption warmly however small, because everyone is watching what happens to whoever goes
first. If the organisers gave a hard stop, say the actual end time out loud here.

**4 · "The best time to plant a tree…" ★new** Fifteen seconds. Say it once, do not explain it, go
straight to the question. It is doing one job: **taking the blame off the table before you present the
evidence.** "Everything I'm about to show you is going to sound like bad news. Nobody here is behind —
the decisions that created this were reasonable when they were made. The only question worth asking is
what you do from today." A defensive room argues with the evidence instead of the conclusion; this is
the cheapest way to keep them open. Most expendable slide in the deck if you start late.

**5 · Ninety days.** Ask it. *"Hands up if you could move your AI workloads to another provider in
ninety days."* Wait. *"Keep them up if you've tested it."* **Wait through the silence — the drop is the
talk.** Then: "Most of us believe we could. Almost none of us have proved it. Anywhere else in the
business we'd call that an untested disaster recovery plan."

**6 · Thesis.** Land slowly. The load-bearing word is **jurisdiction** — not data, not privacy.
"Nobody signed off on a foreign-policy exposure. Somebody signed off on a credit card and a rate
limit. Those turned out to be the same decision."

## 01 The ground moved — 7 min (slides 7–16)

Frame the section: *this is a risk register update, not a philosophical debate.* Facts, no advocacy yet.

**8 · Where you actually are. ★new** The baseline the whole talk sits on. Two numbers, 45 seconds, no
editorialising. "In 2017 European providers had twenty-nine percent of their own market. Today it's
fifteen, and it has been fifteen for three years. The market grew sixfold in that time — the share
halved." **The plateau is the point**, not the decline: a flat line through the loudest three years of
sovereignty rhetoric in European history is what earns you the "procurement, not manifestos" line later.

**9 · Three companies, seventy percent. ★new** 20 seconds, one line, point at the 2%. "Seventy percent
of the European cloud market is three American companies. The largest European provider is two percent.
When people say 'just move to a European alternative' — that is the alternative." This kills the naive
answer early and sets up why the talk is about control planes rather than migrations.

**10 · Microsoft France, under oath.** The strongest slide in the deck because it isn't your claim.
Be scrupulously fair — he also said it has never happened, and the EU Data Boundary has since been
strengthened. "Nobody is accusing anyone of anything. He's describing the structure more honestly
than most of us do in our own risk registers." No US-parented company can contract out of the CLOUD Act.

**11 · Four dates.** 90 seconds, left to right. The arc: *the vendor admitted it → the vendors responded
(AWS, €7.8bn, separate legal entity) → the legal ground under the response moved anyway.* "Three times
in ten years the legal basis for transatlantic transfer has been struck down. If your architecture
assumes it survives a fourth challenge, that's a bet, not a plan."

**12 · The AI Act deadline moved.** Half the room read "delay" and quietly deprioritised. Correct it
gently: "Deferred is not cancelled. The deferral bought you eighteen months on the paperwork — nothing
on the architecture, because the architecture takes longer than the paperwork."

**13 · The rulebook (gantt).** Do **not** read the chart. Point at three things: the red line is today,
everything left of it already binds you, and the one black pin on the Data Act row is the payload —
**from 12 Jan 2027 your provider may not charge you to leave.** "Every sovereignty conversation starts
with 'let's wait and see what Brussels does'. Brussels already did it. This is the past, not the future."

**14 · Six laws.** *Cut first if short.* Deliver the middle column only. "Six laws, one question: can
you show who controls this, and can you walk away." Watch for the financial-services people on DORA.

**15–16 · The numbers.** Read the top one and the bottom one, let the shape do the rest. 93% repatriating
or evaluating; 41% still name public cloud as primary inference, **down from 56% in one year** — that
one is a delta, not a level. If challenged on survey quality, concede it: "Every one has a house bias.
That's why I'm showing you five from five different houses pointing the same way."

## 02 Sovereignty is a dial — 7 min (slides 17–23)

**18 · The framework document. ★new** 20 seconds. Hold it up, do not read the contents list. "This
exists. Version 1.2.1, October last year, from the Commission's own digital directorate. Section three
defines the levels, section five tells you how to compute a score." Its only job is to prove the
framework is operational rather than aspirational — then go straight to the next slide.

**19 · The SEAL scale, 0 to 4. ★rebuilt** Builds one rung per click, ninety seconds. **Do not read all
five** — read 0, 2 and 3 and let the bars do the rest. "Stop arguing about whether you're sovereign.
There's a five-point scale, the Commission scores suppliers against it across eight dimensions, and it
just spent a hundred and eighty million euros using it. Pick a number per workload, write it into the
requirement, and make suppliers answer it."
**The gap between 2 and 3 is the whole talk**: SEAL-2 is what a contract buys you and it fails the
moment a foreign court instructs the parent; SEAL-3 is what an architecture buys you. Same distinction
as the Microsoft testimony on slide 10, expressed as a procurement level.
If challenged that SEAL-4 is unachievable, **agree immediately** — nothing operates there at scale, and
that is why the EU is funding gigafactories. Worth saying out loud: no hyperscaler bid at SEAL-3.

**20 · Four questions.** Jurisdiction, portability, economics, capability — **per workload**, not per
company. "Most organisations have one AI policy and forty AI workloads. That's the mistake."

**21 · What changes.** Read the last line of each column aloud, deliberately. "The right-hand column is
not better. It's different, and it's yours. Anyone who tells you it has no cost is selling it to you."

**22 · The quote wall.** 30 seconds, read only two aloud (von der Leyen's second, Draghi's). Political
will has already become procurement rules and money.

**23 · Who decides the roadmap? ★new** 60 seconds. This pre-empts the protectionism objection before
the debate rather than during it. "There are three ways to answer 'who decides the roadmap'. America
owns the platform. China built its own copy. Europe's bet is that you can stay in the commons and still
have a vote — which is the only one of the three that doesn't require you to be a superpower."
Be fair to the middle card: domestic forks genuinely work, and genuinely cost interoperability.

## 03 The stack is ready — 6 min (slides 24–32)

**Change your voice here.** Sections 1–2 were risk; this is opportunity.

**25 · Boring is the achievement.** 66% already serve inference on Kubernetes; 31 conformant platforms;
llm-d donated to the CNCF by the people selling the alternative. Translate every term. "The reason I can
make this argument in 2026 and couldn't in 2023 is that the hard engineering got commoditised."

**26 · "Open weights" ≠ open model ★new** The honesty beat inside the good-news section — the substrate
is solved, the model layer is not. "Everybody here has been told they can have an open model. You can
download the weights, you cannot audit what went into them, and the licence still says what you may
build. That's portable — it isn't accountable." If challenged that tier 3 is behind the frontier,
**agree without hedging**, then note it is the only tier you can put in front of a regulator alone.
This is why "knowingly" appears in the *rent the frontier* row on slide 38.

**27 · KubeCon sessions.** Show, don't read. 15 seconds. "SAP. NVIDIA. IBM. Red Hat. The German armed
forces. The UN's telecoms agency. Nobody here is a sovereignty activist. They're all just shipping."

**28 · Reference architecture.** 90 seconds, proof of depth not argument. Point at exactly three: the
**gateway**, the **data layer**, and the **bottom layer you cannot download**.

**29–32 · The build-out.** Let them see the megawatt gap before you speak: "The bottom bar is live this
month, twenty kilometres from here. The top bar is the same company in 2027. The constraint isn't chips
or capital — it's a grid connection." **Disclose the VOLT relationship plainly.**
"Every AI strategy in this country eventually becomes an energy strategy."

## 04 What the keynote leaves out — 5 min (slides 33–39)

This is the credibility section. **Do not rush it and do not soften it.**

**34 · Four failure modes.** GPU contention is a governance problem in a Kubernetes costume. "I've
watched a data science team and a product team fight over eight GPUs for six weeks. No technology fixed
it. A quota policy and one uncomfortable meeting fixed it."

**35 · Open source is the floor ★new** The bill that comes with section 03. "The innovation fetish is
unhealthy. Maintain what is there. If you adopt an open stack and fund none of it, you haven't become
sovereign — you've moved your dependency somewhere with no support contract." The ask is small: name
the handful of dependencies you could not replace in a quarter, and put a budget line against each.

**36–37 · Economics.** Same H100: $0.21 to $15.25 per million output tokens. Give explicit permission
not to self-host: "If your AI spend is €4,000 a month and spiky, self-hosting is a hobby. Come back when
it's €40,000 and flat — and most of you will get there faster than you think."

**38 · Self-host / federate / rent.** **Never cut this.** Row two is load-bearing: "You don't have to
leave the hyperscalers to stop being captured by them. You have to own the layer where the decisions
are made."

**39 · People.** *Cut if short.* Two to four people, not a department. "Every question in this talk that
sounds like infrastructure is a hiring question in disguise."

## 05 Close — 2 min (slides 40–42)

**40 · Ninety days, four moves.** Slow right down — this is what goes in the notebook. Inventory →
gateway in front → prove portability once with a real production workload → write a SEAL level into the
next procurement. "None of these need a business case. Three are someone's existing job. The first is a
spreadsheet, and I'd bet nobody here can produce it today."

**41 · "The clouds aren't going anywhere. Your autonomy shouldn't live there."** Pause.
**Do not fill the silence.** Then the principles slide, then the debate.

*Ordering note: 40 is a mic drop and 41 is a summary, so the emotional peak currently lands one slide
early. If it feels deflating in rehearsal, swap them — deliver the principles first and let "your
autonomy shouldn't live there" be the last thing said before the provocations.*

**42 · Sovereignty is not a product. It's a practice. ★new** The last thing you say before the room
takes over — 40 seconds, and read the three middle lines slowly. "If you remember one slide, make it
this one. Sovereignty is not something you buy. There is no product on the market that makes you
sovereign, and anyone selling you one is doing to sovereignty what a decade of marketing did to
sustainability. It's a practice — what you do every quarter, in procurement, in architecture, and in
who you hire." **"Landlord model" is the line that lands**: most of this room was sold EU region
residency as sovereignty, and they know instinctively what it means to improve a property they do not
own. The closing line is a deliberate callback to slide 35. Then stop, advance, and let them start.

## 06 Your turn — 17–23 min (slides 43–48)

**Run the exercise first, then argue.** A room that has just written something down has opinions
grounded in its own workloads; a cold room debates in the abstract and three loud people carry it.

**43 · One workload, four boxes. ★new** Five minutes, and **be quiet while they work.** "Pick one AI
workload you actually run — not the portfolio, one. Four questions on the screen. Five minutes, then
compare with whoever is next to you. I'll be quiet." The first thirty seconds always look unproductive
from the stage; do not rescue it, do not narrate over it, do not start early.
**Box 2 is the whole exercise** — almost nobody can say what leaving would cost in weeks. Bring them
back with a hard "thirty seconds", then ask for a show of hands on who filled in box 2. It is the same
show of hands as slide 5, except now they have proved it to themselves instead of taking your word.
Take three answers, no more, then move on.

**44 · Or start closer to home. ★new** *Backup, not scheduled.* Use it if the exercise finishes early,
if the provocations do not catch, or if you would rather run table discussion than one big-room
argument. The segmentation matters: a founder will not engage with a dependency-maintenance question
and a CTO will not engage with a pricing-escalation one, but each will engage hard with their own.

**45 · Five things I believe.** Leave it up for the rest of the session. "Five things I believe. Pick a number and take it apart."
Honest positions: you half-believe **#1** (slide 23 is your answer to it), strongly believe **#5**.
**If the room is quiet, open #4 yourself** — ask who has tried to hire a platform engineer this year.
If that does not catch either, go back to 44 and put one of the four segmented questions to the room
by name. The unpaid-contributor one never fails: nobody knows the answer, and the not-knowing is the
point.

**46 · ChemAI card.** 15 seconds, then **stop talking** and let them photograph the QR code.
"AI for molecules and materials, Amsterdam, 12–13 November. Photograph it now — the next slide is just
my contact details."

**47 · Close.** Inventory, who I am, the VOLT disclosure one last time, and how to reach me. 15 seconds.

**48 · Sources** is a backup. Only open it if a number is challenged — then find the line and read it.
